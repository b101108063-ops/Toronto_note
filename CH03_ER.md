## Trauma and Resuscitation

### Primary Survey and ABCs

**Hemorrhagic Shock**

Classification of hemorrhagic shock is based on blood loss volume, vital sign changes, and clinical indicators. Class I involves up to 15% blood volume loss with mild tachycardia but normal blood pressure and respiratory rate. Class II presents with 15-30% loss showing tachycardia exceeding 100 bpm and decreased capillary refill. Class III demonstrates 30-40% loss with more pronounced tachycardia above 120 bpm, hypotension, and tachypnea. Class IV represents over 40% blood volume loss with severe hypotension, altered mental status, and anuria. Management priorities include airway protection, hemorrhage control through direct pressure and elevation, intravenous access with large-bore cannulas, permissive hypotension with blood product transfusion, and activation of massive transfusion protocols when indicated.

**Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)**

GCS serves as a critical tool for assessing trauma patients with altered level of consciousness, providing both severity of injury and neurosurgical prognosis indicators. The scale evaluates eye opening (1-4), verbal response (1-5), and motor response (1-6), with a maximum score of 15. Scores of 13-15 indicate mild injury, 9-12 moderate injury, and below 9 severe injury. When patients are intubated, GCS is reported out of 10+T. Deteriorating GCS scores require immediate intervention, and prognosis should be based on best post-resuscitation values. Pupil assessment including equality, size, symmetry, and reactivity to light, along with lateralizing motor deficits, provides additional critical information.

**Fluid Resuscitation Principles**

Initial fluid bolus should be followed by monitoring for improvement in heart rate, blood pressure, and urine output, with early consideration of blood products. Maintenance fluid requirements follow the 4:2:1 rule: 4 mL/kg/h for the first 10 kg, 2 mL/kg/h for the next 10 kg, and 1 mL/kg/h for remaining weight. For patients weighing 20 kg or more, maintenance rate can be calculated as 40 + patient weight in kg. Ongoing losses and deficits must be replaced, assuming a baseline of 10% body weight. Common sites of internal bleeding requiring surgical evaluation include the abdomen, chest, pelvis, long bones, and gastrointestinal tract.

Immediate defibrillation is indicated for shockable rhythms (ventricular fibrillation or ventricular tachycardia). For foreign-body airway obstruction, abdominal thrusts are recommended in conscious adults, while back blows and chest compressions are used in infants.

### Causes of Shock

The differential diagnosis of shock can be remembered using the mnemonic **SHOCKED**: Septic or neurogenic shock; Hemorrhagic shock from bleeding; Obstructive causes including tension pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade, and pulmonary embolism; Cardiogenic etiologies such as blunt myocardial injury, arrhythmia, and myocardial infarction; Anaphylactic shock; Endocrine causes including Addison's crisis, myxedema coma; and Drug-induced shock. Common bleeding sites include external wounds (particularly scalp injuries), chest cavity, abdomen (peritoneal and retroperitoneal spaces), pelvis, and the gastrointestinal tract.

### FAST Examination

The Focused Assessment with Sonography in Trauma examines four key areas. The subxiphoid view assesses the pericardial window for pericardial effusion. The perisplenic view evaluates the spleen, left kidney, and free fluid in the left upper quadrant. The hepatorenal view (Morrison's pouch) checks for blood between the liver and right kidney. The pelvic/retrovesical view examines the bladder and potential free fluid in the pouch of Douglas. A normal FAST shows no free fluid, while a positive FAST indicates the presence of free fluid requiring further evaluation.

### Secondary Survey

The secondary survey begins after primary survey completion, initial resuscitation, and stabilization. History should follow the **SAMPLE** format: Signs and symptoms, Allergies, Medications, Past medical history, Last meal, and Events related to the injury or illness. Physical examination proceeds systematically through head and neck (periorbital bruising, mastoid bruising, ocular injuries, facial bone palpation), chest (equal breath sounds, subcutaneous emphysema), abdomen (peritonitis, distention, tenderness), musculoskeletal (swelling, deformity, pulses, log roll for spine), and neurological systems. Foley catheter insertion is contraindicated when lower urinary tract trauma is suspected, indicated by blood at the urethral meatus, perineal hematoma, or high-riding prostate on digital rectal examination. Nasogastric tube contraindications include significant mid-face trauma and basal skull fracture.

### Ethical Considerations in Emergency Care

**Consent to Treatment - Adults:** The Emergency Rule permits treatment without consent when a patient faces imminent risk from serious injury and obtaining consent is either impossible or would increase risk. Any capable patient may refuse treatment even if life-saving. Substitute decision makers must be identified early. Exceptions to the Emergency Rule include prior refusal of the same treatment, advanced directives such as DNR orders, and suicide attempts where care must still be provided.

**Consent to Treatment - Children:** Immediate treatment is warranted for imminent risk. Parents or guardians make treatment decisions unless the child is an emancipated or mature minor. When parents refuse life-saving treatment, act in the child's best interest while involving child protection services.

**Duty to Report:** Requirements vary by jurisdiction but commonly include gunshot wounds, suspected child abuse, communicable diseases, medical unsuitability to drive, and risk of substantial harm to others.

**Jehovah's Witnesses:** Capable adults may refuse whole blood, packed red blood cells, platelets, and plasma. Albumin, immunoglobulins, and hemophilic preparations should be clarified. For children, if life-saving treatment is refused, act in the child's interest and involve child protection services immediately.

## Traumatology

### Head Trauma

**Assessment of Brain Injury**

Mechanism of injury guides anticipation of injuries. Always inquire about headache, loss of consciousness, amnesia, vomiting, and seizure activity. Assume cervical spine injury until ruled out. Vital signs may reveal shock (not typically from isolated brain injury except in infants) or Cushing's response to increasing intracranial pressure (bradycardia, hypertension, irregular respirations). Severity determination relies on level of consciousness (GCS <8 warrants intubation; any score change of 3 or more indicates serious injury), pupil examination (anisocoria >1 mm in altered consciousness requires attention), and lateralizing signs. High-risk injury mechanisms include high-speed MVC with ejection, motorcycle collisions, pedestrian-vehicle crashes, and falls from height exceeding 0.91 meters.

**Canadian CT Head Rule**

Non-contrast head CT is required for minor head injury patients with any of the following high-risk factors: GCS score less than 15 at 2 hours after injury; suspected open or depressed skull fracture; signs of basal skull fracture; 2 or more episodes of vomiting; and age 65 years or older. Medium-risk factors for brain injury on CT include amnesia before impact exceeding 30 minutes and dangerous mechanisms such as pedestrian struck by motor vehicle, occupant ejected from vehicle, or fall from height greater than 3 feet or five stairs. This rule applies only to trauma cases, not to patients with GCS less than 13, ages 16 and under, or those on anticoagulants with bleeding disorders.

**Signs of Increased Intracranial Pressure**

Deteriorating level of consciousness is the hallmark sign. Respiratory pattern changes and Cushing's reflex (high blood pressure with low heart rate and irregular respirations) indicate severe elevation. Lateralizing central nervous system signs such as cranial nerve palsies and hemiparesis, seizures, papilledema (late finding), nausea, vomiting, and headache are all concerning features.

**Signs of Basal Skull Fracture**

Battle's sign (bruised mastoid process), hemotympanum, raccoon eyes (periorbital bruising), and CSF rhinorrhea or otorrhea suggest basal skull fracture.

**Management of Head Trauma**

Goals focus on reducing secondary injury through prevention of hypoxia, ischemia, and decreased cerebral perfusion pressure. Airway management with intubation and prevention of hypercarbia are essential. Blood pressure should be maintained with systolic pressure above 90 mmHg or mean arterial pressure above 60 mmHg. Early neurosurgical consultation is critical. For seizure treatment or prophylaxis, consider phenytoin or levetiracetam. Suspected raised intracranial pressure management includes neuroprotective rapid sequence intubation with fentanyl or lidocaine pretreatment, sedation or paralysis, hyperventilation targeting pCO2 of 30-35 mmHg, head of bed elevation to 20 degrees, adequate blood pressure for cerebral perfusion, mannitol 1 g/kg, and removal of cervical collar when safe.

**Specific Head Injuries**

Skull fractures include vault fractures (linear non-depressed being most common, often over temporal bone near the middle meningeal artery) and depressed fractures classified as open or closed. Basal skull fractures typically occur through the floor of the anterior cranial fossa. Scalp lacerations can cause significant bleeding and require haemostasis with skull bone defect palpation and CT head to rule out fracture. Diffuse injuries range from mild traumatic brain injury (concussion with GCS 13-15) characterized by confusion and amnesia with loss of consciousness less than 30 minutes and post-traumatic amnesia less than 24 hours, to diffuse axonal injury requiring MRI diagnosis with varying coma duration. Focal injuries include contusions and intracranial hemorrhage (epidural, subdural, intracerebral).

**Potentially Life-Threatening Chest Injuries**

Pulmonary contusion causes interstitial edema impairing compliance and gas exchange, managed with adequate ventilation, monitoring, chest physiotherapy, and positive pressure ventilation if severe. Ruptured diaphragm requires laparotomy for repair and management of intra-abdominal injuries. Esophageal injury typically presents with penetrating trauma or caustic ingestion with pain disproportionate to injury; diagnosis via esophagram and flexible esophagoscopy. Aortic tear occurs at the subclavian artery near the ligamentum arteriosum in 90% of cases, presenting with sudden high-speed deceleration and complaints of chest pain and dyspnea. Blunt myocardial injury is difficult to diagnose in multisystem trauma and is evaluated with ECG, cardiac biomarkers, and echocardiography.

### Abdominal Trauma

**Blunt Trauma**

Solid organ injury (spleen most common, liver second) predominates with blunt mechanism. History should assess mechanism and SAMPLE. Physical examination is often unreliable in multisystem trauma, requiring serial examinations. Signs include tachycardia, tachypnea, oliguria, fever, and hypotension. Seatbelt sign indicates potential retroperitoneal duodenal trauma, intraperitoneal bowel transection, mesenteric injury, and lumbar spine injury. Investigation options include FAST (identifies free fluid but limited for retroperitoneal bleeding), CXR (free air, diaphragmatic hernia), CT scan (most specific), and diagnostic peritoneal lavage (most sensitive but rarely used).

**Management Principles**

Hemodynamically unstable patients require laparotomy. Decision for surgery in solid organ injuries is based on hemodynamic stability rather than specific injuries. Hollow organ injuries generally require laparotomy. Even with low suspicion, admit and observe for 24 hours.

**Penetrating Trauma**

High risk of gastrointestinal perforation, sepsis, and cardiac injury if wound is in the cardiac box. History should clarify the weapon characteristics and trajectory.

### Wound Care

**Wound Classification and Management**

Acute treatment follows RICE principles: Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation. High-risk factors for infection include puncture wounds, crush injuries, wounds over 12 hours old, hand or foot wounds, age over 50, prosthetic joints or valves, and immunocompromised states. Early wound irrigation and debridement are the most important factors in decreasing infection risk. Suture use depends on location: face 6-0/5-0 (5-7 days), joints 2-0 (10-14 days), scalp 4-0 (7-10 days). Sutures are not indicated for delayed presentation over 24 hours, puncture wounds, mammalian bites, crush injuries, or retained foreign bodies. Tetanus prophylaxis is essential. Alternatives include tissue glue, Steri-Strips, and staples.

**Approach to Abdominal Pain**

History follows OPQRST for pain characterization. Physical examination includes ABCs (never delay management in unstable patients), vitals, full abdominal exam including digital rectal examination and CVA tenderness, pelvic/genital exam, respiratory and cardiac exams. Investigations include CBC, electrolytes, glucose, BUN/Cr, urinalysis, liver enzymes, LFTs, lipase, B-hCG, ECG, troponins, VBG/lactate, POCUS for free fluid/AAA/effusions, AXR for foreign body or small bowel obstruction, CXR for free air, and CT for specific indications. Ruptured AAA presents with flank or back pain, pulsatile abdominal mass, and hypotension; screening POCUS is standard for any patient with AAA suspicion.

### Acute Pelvic Pain

**Gynecological Causes**

Ovarian cysts (ruptured most common cause), ovarian abscess, ovarian torsion (50% will have ovarian mass), mittelschmerz; fallopian tube pathology including salpingitis, tubal abscess, hydrosalpinx; uterine causes such as leiomyomas with torsion, PID, cervicitis; ectopic pregnancy; spontaneous abortion; and endometriosis. Non-gynecological causes are extensive. Ultrasound is the preferred imaging modality for acute pelvic pain assessment.

### Altered Level of Consciousness

**Definitions**

Altered mental status encompasses cognitive function changes, behavior, or attentiveness. Delirium involves fluctuating attention and awareness. Dementia is progressive cognitive decline. Lethargy describes decreased awareness with patient appearing wakeful. Stupor indicates unresponsiveness but rousable state. Coma represents unrousable sleep-like state.

**Etiology of Coma**

Using the mnemonic **AEIOU TIPS**: Acidosis/Alcohol; Epilepsy; Infection; Oxygen (hypoxia)/Opiates; Uremia; Temperature/Trauma (especially head); Insulin (too little or too much); Psychogenic/Poisoning; Structural or space-occupying lesions. Causes can be categorized as diffuse bilateral cerebral hemisphere processes (metabolic/toxic disorders) or focal brainstem lesions (compression, infarction, hemorrhage).

### Chest Pain

**Life-Threatening Causes**

Remember **PET MAP**: Pneumothorax; Esophageal rupture; Tamponade; MI/angina; Aortic dissection; Pulmonary embolism.

**HEART Score for Risk Stratification**

History receives 2 points for highly suspicious, 1 for moderately suspicious, 0 for slightly or non-suspicious presentation. ECG findings award 2 points for acute ischemia, 1 for non-specific findings, and 0 for normal. Age contributes 2 points if over 65 years, 1 point for ages 45-65. Risk factors are scored based on the presence of three or more factors. Traditional risk factors for coronary artery disease include hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, smoking, and family history.

### Syncope

Syncope is defined as sudden, transient loss of consciousness and postural tone with spontaneous recovery, usually from generalized cerebral or brainstem hypoperfusion. Cardiogenic causes include dysrhythmia, outflow obstruction, MI, and valvular disease. Non-cardiogenic causes involve peripheral vascular mechanisms, vasovagal responses, orthostatic hypotension, CNS pathology, and metabolic disturbances. Key historical features include prodrome, situation and setting, and distinguishing syncope from seizure (seizure has postictal period; myoclonic jerks suggest syncope, not seizure). ECG should assess for tachycardia, bradycardia, blocks, Wolff-Parkinson White, long QT interval, Brugada Syndrome, and other conditions.

### Headache

**Ottawa SAH Rule**

Investigate for subarachnoid hemorrhage in alert patients over 5 years with new severe non-traumatic headache reaching maximum intensity within 1 hour if any high-risk variable is present: age over 40 years, neck pain or stiffness, witnessed loss of consciousness, onset during exertion, thunderclap headache, or limited neck flexion on examination. This rule has 100% sensitivity for identifying SAH.

**Red Flags for Severe Headache**

Meningitis presents with fever, neck stiffness, and altered mental status. Giant cell arteritis causes headache, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication, and visual symptoms in patients over 70 years. Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis risk factors include neoplasm, pregnancy, systemic diseases, dehydration, oral contraceptives, and coagulopathies.

### Joint Pain

Rule out life-threatening causes such as septic joint. History should assess trauma, drug use, fever, constitutional symptoms, skin lesions, and conjunctivitis. Evaluate patterns of joint involvement (polyarticular vs. monoarticular, symmetric vs. asymmetric) and inflammatory symptoms (morning stiffness over 30 minutes, pain easing with activity). Physical examination assesses range of motion, localized pain, effusion, erythema, warmth, and inability to bear weight. Investigations include CBC, ESR, CRP, INR/PTT, blood cultures, joint X-ray with contralateral comparison, bedside ultrasound for effusion, and joint aspiration for culture, WBC, and crystals.

### Back Pain

**Red Flags**

Bowel or bladder dysfunction, saddle anesthesia, constitutional symptoms, chronic disease with constant pain, paresthesia, age over 50 with mild trauma, IV drug use or infection, and neuromotor deficits require urgent evaluation. Rule out extraspinal emergencies including aortic dissection, AAA, PE, MI, retroperitoneal bleed, and pancreatitis. Spinal emergencies to exclude are osteomyelitis, cauda equina syndrome, epidural abscess or hematoma, discitis, spinal fracture, and malignancy.

### Seizures

Status epilepticus is defined as continuous or intermittent seizure activity for greater than 5 minutes without regaining consciousness. Generalized seizures always involve loss of consciousness. Partial seizures may be simple or complex. Common causes include primary seizure disorder, structural lesions (trauma, intracranial hemorrhage, infection, increased ICP), and metabolic disturbances. Status epilepticus management requires immediate airway protection with positioning and intubation if compromised, monitoring with vitals and ECG, establishing IV access, and administering benzodiazepines (lorazepam 4-10 mg IV or midazolam 5-10 mg IM, repeat at 10 minutes if ineffective).

## Cardiovascular Emergencies

### Acute Coronary Syndrome

**Definition and Classification**

Acute coronary syndrome encompasses new-onset chest pain, acute worsening of previous pain, or pain at rest with negative cardiac biomarkers and no ECG changes indicating unstable angina; positive biomarkers with NSTEMI changes; or positive biomarkers with STEMI. Occlusion myocardial infarction represents a new paradigm supporting ECG findings suggesting acute coronary occlusion even without ST elevation, including Wellens syndrome, De Winter T waves, and hyperacute T waves.

**Management**

Stabilization begins with ABCs, oxygen, IV access, and cardiac monitors. Aspirin 162-325 mg should be chewed and swallowed. Nitroglycerin 0.3 mg sublingually every 5 minutes for three doses is appropriate for persistent pain, CHF, or hypertension, but is contraindicated in hypotension, phosphodiesterase inhibitor use, and right ventricular infarctions. Anticoagulation and additional antiplatelet therapy depend on STEMI versus NSTEMI and reperfusion strategy. Early cardiology consultation is essential for reperfusion therapy. Primary percutaneous coronary intervention is preferred for STEMI within 90 minutes; thrombolytics are an alternative if PCI is unavailable within 120 minutes.

### Heart Failure

**Precipitants of Acute Decompensated Heart Failure**

Remember **FAILURE**: Forgot medication; Arrhythmia (dysrhythmia) or Anemia; Ischemia/Infarction/Infection; Lifestyle (high salt intake); Upregulation of cardiac output (pregnancy, hyperthyroidism); Renal failure; Embolism (pulmonary).

**CXR Findings in CHF**

Pulmonary vascular redistribution, perihilar infiltrates, interstitial edema with Kerley B lines, alveolar edema with bilateral infiltrates, cardiomegaly, pleural effusions (usually right-sided), peribronchial cuffing, and fissural thickening indicate heart failure.

**Acute Treatment**

The **LMNOP** approach: Lasix (furosemide); Morphine; Nitroglycerin; Oxygen; Position (sit upright) and Pressure (Bi-PAP).

### Tachydysrhythmias

**Narrow QRS (Supraventricular)**

Atrial fibrillation management includes hemodynamic compromise requiring synchronized cardioversion; no compromise allows rate control with beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, digoxin, or amiodarone, plus rhythm control with cardioversion and consideration of anticoagulation. Chemical cardioversion uses procainamide, flecainide, or propafenone. Long-term management involves rate or rhythm control and anticoagulation based on CHA2DS2-VASc score. Remember the **PIRATES** causes: Pulmonary embolism; Ischemic heart disease; Rheumatic or valvular disease; Anemia; Thyroid (hyperthyroidism); EtOH and Elevated blood pressure; Sick sinus or Stress (surgery, sepsis).

**Wide QRS (Ventricular)**

Ventricular tachycardia (VTach) is defined as 3 or more consecutive ventricular beats at over 100 bpm, usually 140-200 bpm, most commonly from CAD with MI. Sustained VTach exceeding 30 seconds is an emergency. With hemodynamic compromise, perform synchronized DC cardioversion. Without compromise, use synchronized cardioversion, amiodarone, or procainamide. Ventricular fibrillation requires code blue and ACLS for pulseless arrest. Torsades de pointes appears like VTach but with QRS rotating around baseline; causes include prolonged QT from drugs (quinidine, TCAs, erythromycin, quinolones), electrolyte imbalance, and congenital conditions. Treatment involves IV magnesium, temporary overdrive pacing, isoproterenol, and correction of the underlying cause.

**Definition**

Severe blood pressure elevation with evidence of end-organ damage affecting CNS, retina, cardiovascular system, kidneys, or gastrointestinal tract.

**Management Strategy**

Reduce blood pressure gradually and progressively, aiming for 25% reduction over the initial 60 minutes. For preeclampsia, consult OB/GYN immediately and administer magnesium sulfate (4 g IV bolus over 20 minutes, then 1 g/hour maintenance). Establish arterial line if encephalopathic and transfer to ICU. For aortic dissection, rapidly reduce systolic blood pressure to 110-120 mmHg. Avoid beta-blockers with catecholamine excess (except labetalol).

### Venous Thromboembolism

**Virchow's Triad**

Alterations in blood flow (venous stasis), injury to endothelium (smoking, HTN, surgery, catheter, trauma), and hypercoagulable state (pregnancy, oral contraceptive use, malignancy).

**Wells' Criteria for DVT**

Active cancer (1 point); paralysis, paresis, or recent immobilization of leg (1 point); recently bedridden or major surgery within 4 weeks (1 point); local tenderness along deep venous system (1 point); entire leg swollen (1 point); calf swelling greater than 3 cm compared to asymptomatic leg (1 point); unilateral pitting edema (1 point); collateral superficial veins (1 point); alternative diagnosis as likely or more likely (-2 points). Score 0-1 indicates low probability; 2 moderate probability; 3 or higher high probability.

**Wells' Criteria for PE**

Previous history of DVT/PE (1.5 points); HR greater than 100 (1.5 points); recent immobilization or surgery (1.5 points); clinical signs of DVT (3 points); alternative diagnosis less likely than PE (3 points); hemoptysis (1 point); cancer (1 point). Score below 2 suggests low probability; 2-6 intermediate probability; over 6 high probability.

**DVT Management**

Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) including rivaroxaban 15 mg PO BID for 21 days then 20 mg daily, or apixaban 10 mg BID for 7 days then 5 mg BID, are options. Low molecular weight heparin (dalteparin 200 IU/kg SC daily or enoxaparin 1 mg/kg SC daily) is preferred unless renal failure is present. Consider thrombolysis for extensive DVT threatening limb. IVC filter or surgical thrombectomy if anticoagulation is contraindicated.

### Sepsis

**Definitions**

Systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) requires 2 or more criteria: temperature over 38C or below 36C; HR over 90; RR over 20; WBC over 12. Sepsis is SIRS with suspected or confirmed infection. Septic shock is sepsis with initial lactate over 4 mmol/L or hypotension. qSOFA score greater than 2 indicates high risk for in-hospital mortality but should not be used as a single screening tool.

**Surviving Sepsis Campaign 1-Hour Bundle**

Measure lactate level and remeasure if initially over 2 mmol/L. Obtain blood cultures before antibiotic administration. Administer broad-spectrum antibiotics. Begin rapid administration of 30 mL/kg crystalloid for hypotension or lactate over 4 mmol/L. Apply vasopressors if hypotensive during or after fluid resuscitation to maintain MAP over 65 mmHg.

## Respiratory Emergencies

### Acute Exacerbation of COPD

**Definition**

Progressive development of irreversible airway obstruction, typically caused by smoking. Cardinal symptoms of AECOPD include increased dyspnea, increased coughing frequency or severity, and increased sputum volume or purulence.

**Physical Exam Findings**

Wheeze, maximum laryngeal height less than 4 cm, forced expiratory time over 6 seconds, decreased breath sounds, and decreased cardiac dullness.

**Management**

Maintain oxygen saturation at 88-92%. Administer short-acting beta-agonist (salbutamol 4-8 puffs via MDI with spacer every 15 minutes times 3) plus short-acting anticholinergic (ipratropium 4-8 puffs via MDI every 15 minutes times 3). Give prednisone 40-60 mg PO for 7-14 days, or methylprednisolone 1-2 mg/kg IV if severe exacerbation or unable to take orally. Antibiotics are indicated if all 3 cardinal symptoms are present or 2 cardinal symptoms with increased purulence, using trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, cephalosporins, or respiratory quinolones. Non-invasive positive-pressure ventilation (CPAP or Bi-PAP) is indicated for severe distress, pH below 7.35, or hypercapnia. Intubation may be necessary for life-threatening cases.

### Pulmonary Embolism

**Presentation**

Dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, hemoptysis, tachypnea, cyanosis, hypoxia, fever. Clinical signs and symptoms are unreliable for diagnosis.

**ECG Changes**

Sinus tachycardia, S1Q3T3 pattern, T wave inversions in anterior and inferior leads, and possible AFib.

**CXR Findings**

Hampton's hump (triangular density from pleura indicating pulmonary infarct) and Westermark's sign (vessel dilatation proximal to obstruction with collapse distally).

**Management**

Thrombolysis is indicated for massive PE with sustained hypotension (systolic BP below 90 mmHg for at least 15 minutes or requiring inotropic support). Catheter-directed thrombolysis or surgical thrombectomy may be considered for massive PE or contraindicated anticoagulation.

## Endocrine Emergencies

### Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA)

**Definition**

Triad of hyperglycemia, ketosis, and acidosis from severe insulin deficiency with counter-regulatory hormone excess. Precipitating factors include infection, ischemia, infarction, intoxication, insulin omission, and SGLT2 inhibitors.

**Signs and Symptoms**

Polyuria, polydipsia, malaise, nocturia, weight loss, abdominal pain (early); nausea, vomiting, increasing abdominal pain, Kussmaul respirations, fruity ketotic breath, fatigue, drowsiness, stupor, coma (late).

**Criteria for Diagnosis**

Hyperglycemia (blood glucose usually 14-55 mmol/L), metabolic acidosis (pH below 7.3, bicarbonate below 18 mEq/L), and hyperketonemia or ketonuria.

**Management**

Rehydrate with bolus normal saline then high-rate NS infusion. Replace potassium (only if patient is voiding, adequate renal function, and K+ below 5.5 mmol/L) using 20-40 mEq/L KCl. Insulin is critical to inhibit gluconeogenesis and ketosis; do not give if K+ below 3.3 mmol/L; continuous infusion at 0.05-0.1 U/kg/h; add dextrose when glucose falls below 14 mmol/L. Bicarbonate only if pH below 7.0 with shock or impending death.

### Hyperosmolar Hyperglycemic State (HHS)

**Definition**

Extreme hyperglycemia (often over 33.3 mmol/L) with relative insulin deficiency, counter-regulatory hormone excess, gluconeogenesis, and dehydration. More common in older T2DM patients with symptoms evolving over days to weeks.

**Clinical Features**

Mental disturbances, coma, delirium, seizures, polyuria, nausea/vomiting. Fewer GI symptoms but more neurological deficits than DKA.

### Hypoglycemia

**Whipple's Triad**

Low plasma glucose, symptoms suggestive of hypoglycemia, and prompt resolution with glucose administration.

**Clinical Features**

Neuroglycopenic symptoms: headaches, confusion, seizures, loss of consciousness, coma. Autonomic symptoms: diaphoresis, nausea, tremor, hunger, tachycardia, palpitations.

**Management**

Check bedside capillary glucose. Give oral sugar if possible. Establish IV access. Administer D50W 50 mL IV push (use lower concentration dextrose solutions in children). If IV and oral routes unavailable, give glucagon 1-2 mg IM. Provide oxygen, cardiac monitoring, and frequent glucose checks. Give thiamine 300 mg IM if alcohol use disorder suspected. Search for underlying cause.

## Stroke and Transient Ischemic Attack

**Definitions**

Stroke is sudden loss of brain function due to ischemia (87%) or hemorrhage (13%) with symptoms persisting over 24 hours or neuroimaging evidence. TIA is transient neurologic dysfunction from focal ischemia without acute infarction.

**Clinical Features**

<table>
  <tr><th>Category</th><th>Findings</th></tr>
  <tr><td>General</td><td>Decreased LOC (rare), confusion, neglect, altered mental status</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Language</td><td>Dysarthria, aphasia</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Vision</td><td>Diplopia, eye deviation, visual field defect</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Coordination</td><td>Ataxia, intention tremor, lack of coordination</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Motor</td><td>Increased tone, loss of power, flaccid paralysis</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Sensation</td><td>Loss of sensation</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Reflex</td><td>Hyperreflexia, clonus</td></tr>
</table>

**Stroke Syndromes**

Anterior cerebral artery: contralateral hemianesthesia and hemiparesis (legs greater than arms), gait apraxia, altered mental status. Middle cerebral artery: contralateral hemianesthesia and hemiparesis (arms/face greater than legs), contralateral homonymous hemianopsia, ipsilateral gaze preference. Posterior cerebral artery: contralateral homonymous hemianopsia, cortical blindness, impaired memory. Vertebrobasilar artery: facial hypoesthesia, syncope, ataxia, potentially locked-in syndrome.

**Management**

Thrombolysis with rt-PA (alteplase, TNK) requires acute onset within 4.5 hours of last seen normal, compatible physical findings, and no hemorrhage on CT. Thrombectomy may be an option in some centers within 4.5-6 hours or up to 24 hours. Dual antiplatelet therapy for 21 days should be initiated for high-risk TIA. Control blood pressure only if severe (systolic over 200 mmHg, diastolic over 120 mmHg, MAP over 140 mmHg). Maintain glycemic control targeting 7.8-10 mmol/L.

## Otolaryngological Emergencies

### Epistaxis

**Etiology**

Ninety percent of nosebleeds originate from the anterior nasal septum (Kiesselbach's plexus in Little's area). Causes include trauma (digital, blunt, foreign bodies), barometric changes, nasal dryness, chemicals (including cocaine), and systemic disease (coagulopathies, hypertension).

**Management**

Goals are to localize bleeding and achieve haemostasis. First aid includes leaning forward, pinching the cartilaginous portion of the nose for 20 minutes twice. If first aid fails twice, proceed to packing or cautery. Anterior packing involves clearing clots, applying topical anesthesia with vasoconstrictors, and inserting Vaseline gauze or commercial nasal tampon. If packing both nares, give prophylactic anti-staphylococcal antibiotics to prevent sinusitis or toxic shock syndrome. Posterior packing requires ENT consultation and usually admission.

### Dizziness and Vertigo

Distinguish four types: vertigo (room spinning), lightheadedness (disconnected from environment), presyncope (almost blacking out), and dysequilibrium (unstable, off-balance). Rule out stroke and consider adverse drug events.

### Otalgia

Differential includes infections (acute otitis externa, acute otitis media, mastoiditis, malignant otitis externa in diabetics, herpes), trauma, TMJ dysfunction, and referred pain from neck and jaw.

### Hearing Loss

Differentiate conductive versus sensorineural hearing loss. Sudden sensorineural hearing loss is a medical emergency requiring high-dose steroids and urgent referral. Unilateral tinnitus or SSNHL in elderly patients must be presumed acoustic neuroma until proven otherwise.

### Selected Ophthalmologic Emergencies

**Acute Angle Closure Glaucoma:** Unilateral red, painful eye with decreased visual acuity, halos around lights, fixed mid-dilated pupil, marked increase in intraocular pressure (over 40 mmHg), and shallow anterior chamber. Management includes ophthalmology consultation for laser iridotomy and medications: alpha-agonist (epinephrine), alpha-2-agonist (apraclonidine), beta-blocker (timolol), cholinomimetic (pilocarpine), diuretic (acetazolamide, mannitol), prostaglandin analog (latanoprost).

**Corneal Abrasion:** Pain, redness, tearing, photophobia, foreign body sensation with area staining with fluorescein. Most clear spontaneously within 2-48 hours. Remove foreign body under magnification if present.

**Chemical Burn:** Known exposure to acids or alkali (alkali burns are worse). Copious irrigation with normal saline until neutral pH achieved. Cycloplegic drops and topical antibiotics.

**Retinal Artery Occlusion:** Sudden, painless, monocular vision loss with relative afferent pupillary defect. Cherry red spot and retinal pallor. Management focuses on restoring blood flow within 2 hours via globe massage, decreasing intraocular pressure, and ophthalmology consultation.

**Retinal Detachment:** Flashes of light, floaters, and curtains of blackness or peripheral vision loss with painless, progressive course. Loss of red reflex and decreased intraocular pressure may be present. POCUS can identify retinal detachment.

## Gynecologic and Urologic Emergencies

### Vaginal Bleeding

**Etiology in Pregnancy**

First/Second trimester: ectopic pregnancy, abortion (threatened, incomplete, complete, missed, inevitable, septic), molar pregnancy, implantation bleeding, friable cervix, subchorionic hemorrhage. Second/Third trimester: placenta previa, placental abruption, premature rupture of membranes, preterm labour. Postpartum hemorrhage causes follow the **Four T's**: Tone (uterine atony), Tissue (retained placenta), Trauma (laceration, uterine inversion), and Thrombin (coagulopathy).

**Postpartum Hemorrhage Management**

Manage ABCs with 2 large bore IVs, type and cross 4 units blood, immediate OB/GYN consultation. For uterine atony, give oxytocin 10 units IM or 40 units in 1L wide open until uterus is firm. Genital tract trauma requires visual inspection and repair. Retained products need bimanual or speculum evaluation. Consider TXA and tamponade with Bakri tube or Foley catheter if other measures fail.

### Nephrolithiasis (Renal Colic)

**Clinical Features**

Urinary obstruction causes severe colicky pain at flank, groin, testes, or penile tip. Patients exhibit writhing, nausea/vomiting, hematuria (90% microscopic), diaphoresis, tachycardia, and tachypnea. Kidney stones are 80% calcium oxalate, 10% struvite, 10% uric acid.

**Indications for Admission**

Intractable pain, fever suggesting infection, single kidney with ureteral obstruction, bilateral obstructing stones, intractable vomiting, compromised renal function. Remember that obstruction plus infection constitutes a urological emergency requiring urgent consultation, IV fluids, and broad-spectrum antibiotics.

## Dermatologic Emergencies

### Staphylococcal Scalded Skin Syndrome (SSSS)

Caused by exotoxin from coagulase-positive Staphylococcus aureus, mostly in children. Presents with prodrome of fever, irritability, malaise, skin tenderness followed by sudden diffuse erythema with flaccid bullae and desquamation in large sheets.

### Stevens-Johnson Syndrome (SJS) and Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis (TEN)

Drug-induced (phenytoin, sulfas, penicillins, NSAIDs), bone marrow transplant, or blood product transfusion. Presents in adults with diffuse erythema progressing to necrosis, severe mucous membrane blistering, and entire epidermis desquamation with high mortality (over 50% for TEN). Classification by body surface area: SJS under 10%, SJS/TEN overlap 10-30%, TEN over 30%.

**Management**

SJS and TEN require transfer to burn center early. Supportive care for SSSS. For all conditions, treat underlying cause and provide supportive care.

## Environmental Injuries

### Heat-Related Emergencies

**Heat Exhaustion**

Clinical features relate to loss of circulating volume from heat stress with "water depletion" or "salt depletion" presentations. Symptoms include non-specific malaise, nausea, headache, fatigue, and possible dehydration with orthostatic hypotension. Treatment involves rest in cool environment and fluid or electrolyte replacement.

**Heat Stroke**

Life-threatening emergency from failure of compensatory heat-shedding mechanisms. Classified as classical (occurs with high ambient temperatures, patients often older and sedentary) or exertional (high endogenous heat production overwhelming homeostatic mechanisms, patients often younger and active). Body temperature usually over 40.5 degrees Celsius. Altered mental status, seizures, delirium, or coma indicate CNS dysfunction. Management requires immediate cooling with water mist and fans, ice water immersion if available, and airway management. Avoid alpha-agonists and antipyretics.

### Hypothermia

**Classification**

Mild (32-34.9 degrees Celsius): tachypnea, tachycardia, ataxia, dysarthria, shivering. Moderate (28-31.9 degrees Celsius): loss of shivering, dysrhythmias, Osborne (J) waves on ECG, decreased LOC, combative behavior, muscle rigidity, dilated pupils. Severe (below 28 degrees Celsius): unresponsive, hypotension, acidemia, VFib, AF, asystole, apnea.

**Rewarming Options**

Gentle fluid and electrolyte replacement for all patients. Oral fluids if conscious and moving. Passive external rewarming for stable patients with core temperature over 32.2 degrees Celsius. Active external rewarming with warming blankets, but beware of "afterdrop" phenomenon. Active core rewarming for core temperature below 32.2 degrees or cardiovascular instability, including warmed humidified oxygen, IV fluids, peritoneal dialysis, gastric/colonic/pleural/bladder irrigation, and ECMO for most effective and fastest rewarming.

### Frostbite

**Classification by Depth**

Grade 1: absence of initial lesion. Grade 2: initial lesion on distal phalanx. Grade 3: initial lesion on intermediary and proximal phalanx. Grade 4: initial lesion on carpal/tarsal.

**Management**

Treat for hypothermia first. Remove wet and constrictive clothing. Immerse in 37-39 degrees Celsius agitated water for 10-30 minutes with adequate analgesia. Clean injured area and leave open to air. Consider aspiration or debridement of blisters. Tetanus prophylaxis. Antibiotics for high infection risk. Surgical intervention for compartment syndrome from restrictive eschars. Never allow thawed area to refreeze. Consider tPA and iloprost for severe burns.

### Burns

**Burn Size and Depth**

Rule of nines calculates total body surface area. Superficial (first degree) involves epidermis only, painful and tender. Superficial partial thickness (second degree) extends to epidermis and superficial dermis with blister formation, very painful. Deep partial thickness (second degree) involves hair follicles and sebaceous glands with white to yellow, insensate dermis. Full thickness (third degree) destroys epidermis and all dermal layers, appearing pale, insensate, charred or leathery. Deep fourth degree involves fat, muscle, and even bone.

**Management**

Remove noxious agent and establish airway if needed (indicated for burns over 40% BSA, smoke inhalation injury, or signs of upper airway burns). Resuscitation for second and third degree burns with IV access. Fluid boluses if unstable, then Parkland Formula: Ringer's lactate 4 cc/kg/% BSA burned with half given in first 8 hours and half in next 16 hours. Urine output should be 40-50 cc/h or 0.5 cc/kg/h. Pain relief with continuous opioid infusion.

### Inhalation Injury

**Etiology**

Carbon monoxide or cyanide poisoning, direct thermal injury limited to upper airway above vocal cords, and smoke causing bronchospasm and edema.

**Clinical Features**

Cherry red skin and bitter almond odor suggest cyanide toxicity (though often absent). Singed nasal hairs, soot on oral/nasal membranes, sooty sputum, hoarseness, stridor, dyspnea, decreased LOC, and confusion. Pulse oximetry may read normal despite CO poisoning.

**Management**

CO poisoning requires 100% oxygen and possibly hyperbaric oxygen. Cyanide poisoning is treated with hydroxocobalamin 70 mg/kg IV over 15 minutes (maximum 5 g) up to two doses. Direct thermal injury needs humidified oxygen, early intubation, pulmonary toilet, bronchodilators, and mucolytics.

### Non-Fatal Drowning

Most common in children under 4 years and teenagers. Causes lung damage, hypoxemia, and may lead to hypoxic encephalopathy. Assess for shock, cervical spine injuries, hypothermia, and scuba-related injuries.

**Management**

ABCs with treatment for trauma, shock, and hypothermia. Cardiac and oxygen monitors, IV access. Intensive respiratory care with ventilator assistance if needed. Treat dysrhythmias (usually respond to correction of hypoxemia, hypothermia, and acidosis). Control vomiting, seizures, and bronchospasm. Continue CPR until patient is fully rewarmed.

### Warfarin Overdose Protocol

For INR 5-9 with no risk factors: hold warfarin 1-2 days and reduce maintenance dose, or give Vitamin K 1-2 mg PO if increased bleeding risk. For any INR with life-threatening bleeding or planned surgery within 6 hours: hold warfarin and give prothrombin complex concentrate (Octaplex or Beriplex) plus Vitamin K 10 mg IV over 10 minutes.

## Alcohol-Related Emergencies

### Acute Intoxication

**Symptoms:** Slurred speech, CNS depression, disinhibition, lack of coordination, nystagmus, diplopia, dysarthria, ataxia progressing to coma. **Signs:** Hypotension with reflex tachycardia, hypothermia, hypoglycemia. Alcohol levels correlate poorly with intoxication. Management includes considering thiamine 100 mg to prevent Wernicke's encephalopathy, keeping patient until GCS 15 and safe ambulation, and ruling out head trauma if obtunded.

### Alcohol Withdrawal

**Timeline and Syndromes**

8-6 hours: mild withdrawal with generalized tremor, anxiety, agitation, autonomic hyperactivity, insomnia, nausea/vomiting. 12-48 hours: alcoholic hallucinations (visual most common), usually with normal vitals. Greater than 48 hours: withdrawal seizures (brief generalized tonic-clonic seizures, may have several). 3-5 days: delirium tremens in 5% of untreated patients with severely confused state, fluctuating LOC, agitation, hallucinations, tremor, tachycardia, hyperpyrexia, diaphoresis, and high mortality.

**Treatment**

Goal is early treatment to prevent seizures and DT. Lorazepam 2-4 mg IV/PO every 1-4 hours or diazepam 10-20 mg IV/PO every 1-4 hours until CIWA score below 10. Give thiamine 200 mg IM/IV then 50-100 mg daily. Add phenobarbital if no response to benzos. Consider magnesium sulfate 4 g IV if hypomagnesemic. Admit patients with DT or multiple seizures despite treatment.

### Wernicke's Encephalopathy

Oculomotor abnormalities (nystagmus, lateral gaze palsy, disconjugate gaze), cerebellar dysfunction, and global confusion from chronic thiamine deficiency. Prevent with thiamine supplementation.

### Alcoholic Ketoacidosis

Metabolic acidosis with urine ketones, low glucose, and normal osmolality. History of chronic alcohol intake with abrupt decrease or cessation. Malnourished patients with abdominal pain and nausea/vomiting. Treatment involves thiamine before dextrose, dextrose, and volume repletion.

## Psychiatric Emergencies

### Approach to Common Psychiatric Presentations

**Before seeing the patient:** Ensure your own safety; have security or police available if necessary.

**History**

Assess safety including suicidality (ideation, intent, plan, lethal means, past history) and homicidality (ideation, access to weapons, intended victim, history of violence). Identify current stressors, mood, and coping strategies. Assess psychotic symptoms (delusions, hallucinations, disorganized speech, catatonic behavior, negative symptoms). Document substance use history and past psychiatric history including medications and admissions.

**Acute Psychosis - Management**

Remain calm and reassuring. Ensure staff and patient safety with extra staff or security if needed. For escalating agitation, consider physical restraint and chemical restraint. Offer medication rather than administering with force when possible: lorazepam 2 mg PO/IM/SL, olanzapine 5-10 mg PO/IM, or haloperidol 5 mg PO/IM. Treat underlying medical condition and involve psychiatry or Crisis Intervention Team.

### Suicidal Patient

**Epidemiology**

Attempted suicide is more common in females; completed suicide is more common in males. Second leading cause of death in people under 24 years. Significantly increased incidence among marginalized communities.

**Assessment Tools**

SAD PERSONS scale and Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale assess suicide risk.

## Disposition from the Emergency Department

**General Principles**

Admission decisions should be based on the underlying diagnosis, severity, and stability of the patient. Discharge requires resolution of acute symptoms, ability to tolerate oral intake, adequate analgesia, and appropriate follow-up arranged.

**Specific Guidance**

For alcohol-related presentations: ensure stable vitals, ability to walk unassisted, and full orientation before discharge. For methanol or ethylene glycol poisoning: admit for further management. For TCA poisoning with prolonged cardiotoxicity: admit to monitored bed. For hydrocarbon or smoke inhalation: observe for 6-8 hours for delayed pneumonitis. For oral hypoglycemics: admit for minimum 24 hours if hypoglycemic and 12 hours after last octreotide dose. For opioids: administer naloxone and observe for 24 hours; admit if naloxone infusion needed.
