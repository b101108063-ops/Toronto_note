Subglottic Stenosis

Congenital
+ diameter of subglottis <4 mm in neonate (due to thickening of soft tissue of subglottic space or
maldevelopment of cricoid cartilage), or smaller than average size for age as determined by Cotton-Myer
rading system (I: <= 50% obstruction; II: 51-70%; III: 71-99%; IV: no detectable lumen)
+ frequently associated with syndromes (22q11 deletion, trisomy 21, CHARGE)

Acquired
+ following prolonged, repeated, or traumatic intubation
= most commonly due to endotracheal intubation; nasal intubation is less traumatic and preferred in
long-term intubation, as it puts less pressure on the subglottis (tube sits at different orientation) and
there is less movement
«= subglottic stenosis is related to duration of intubation and endotracheal tube size resulting in pressure
necrosis and subsequent scar formation
+ canalso be due to foreign body, infection (e.g. TB, diphtheria, syphilis), or chemical irritation

Clinical Features

+ biphasic stridor

+ respiratory distress

+ recurrent/prolonged croup

Diagnosis
«rigid laryngoscopy and bronchoscopy done under general anesthesia

Treatment
+ ifsoft stenosis: divide tissue with knife or laser, dilate with balloon + steroids
+ iffirm stenosis: laryngotracheoplasty

Laryngomalacia

Definition

* congenital softening of tissues above the glottis, resulting in collapse of the tissues into the glottis and
inspiratory stridor

* anatomical (immature laryngeal anatomy with poor support/tone) or neurological (underdeveloped CNS
for larynx function and tone)

+ cycle of airway obstruction with indrawing of supraglottis on inspiration > negative intrathoracic pressure
from breathing against closed glottis > laryngopharyngeal reflux of acid > increased supraglottic edema
and further obstruction

Clinical Features

+ high-pitched inspiratory stridor at 1-2 wk

+ stridor is constant or intermittent and more pronounced with feeding, crying, agitation, being in supine
position, or following an URTI

+ usually mild, but can be associated wit cyanosis or feeding difficulties when severe leading to failure to thrive

+ common laryngeal findings: omega-shaped epiglottis, short aryepiglottic folds, redundant mucosa over arytenoids

Treatment

observation + proton pump inhibitor (to break the acid reflux cycle that leads to edema and worse airway
obstruction) is usually sufficient, as symptoms spontaneously subside by 12-18 mo in >90% of cases
if severe (10%), supraglottoplasty (division of short aryepiglottic folds +/- remove excess arytenoid mucosa)
provides relief

Foreign Body

Ingested
+ usually stuck at cricopharyngeus muscle

+ coins, toys, batteries Temergency)

+ presents with drooling, dysphagia, stridor if very large

Aspirated
+ usually stuck at right main bronchus
+ peanuts, hard candy, whole grapes, carrot, apple core, popcorn, balloons
+ presentation
« stridor if lodged in trachea (beware of the silent child as there may be complete obstruction)
= unilateral “asthma” if bronchial, therefore often misdiagnosed as asthma
= wheeze, decreased breath sounds, stridor
= ifcompletely occluded airway: cough, lobar pneumonia, atelectasis, mediastinal shift, pneumothorax, death

Diagnosis and Treatment

sudden onset, not necessarily febrile or elevated WBC

any patient with suspected foreign body should be kept NPO immediately

older patient: inspiratory-expiratory chest x-ray (if patient is stable)

younger patient: right and left decubitus chest x-rays. Lack of lung deflation while resting on dependent side
suggests foreign body blocking bronchus

bronchoscopy or esophagoscopy with removal
