prior to starting IV fluids, serum electrolyte values should be measured
in children, all maintenance fluids should have a dextrose component due to their higher risk of
hypoglycemia, especially if they are NPO
common IV fluid combinations used in pediatrics:
= IV normal saline fluid bolus for children with significant hypovolemia
= for maintenance:
+ newborn: D1OW (dextrose 10% in water)
+ first mo of life: D5W/0.45 NS (dextrose 5% in water and 0.45% sodium chloride)+ KCI 20 mEq/L
(only add KCI if voiding well)
« children without special considerations: D5W/NS + KCl 20 mEq/L - decreased risk of
hyponatremia
most important thing to remember when correcting Na* aberrations due to fluid deficits
« risk of cerebral edema with rapid rehydration with hypotonic or isotonic solutions (i.e. NS)
therefore replace fluid slowly with close monitoring
aim to adjust (increase or decrease) plasma [Na’] by no more than 12 mmol/L/d
+ management depends on etiology, severity of symptoms, and timing (acute vs. chronic)

6) Continue to monitor fluid and electrolyte status

. accurate monitoring of daily fluid intake (PO and IV) and ongoing losses (urine output, diarrhea, emesis,
rains)

if child receiving >50% of maintenance fluids through IV, serum electrolyte values should be monitored

daily and therapy adjusted accordingly

avoid iatrogenic hyper/hyponatremia, keep the possibility of SIADH in mind (indicated by hyponatremia

and concentrated urine)

Gastroenterology

Vomiting

History
characteristic of emesis (e.g. projectile, bilious, bloody, etc.)
pattern of emesis (e.g. association with feeds, cyclic, morning, prolonged, positional, etc.)
associated symptoms (e.g. anorexia, diarrhea, abdominal pain, hematochezia, fever, headache, etc.)
sick contacts
history of chronic and recurrent infections
red flags:

= nonspecific symptoms: prolonged vomiting (>12 h in neonates, >24 h in children < 2 yr, >48 hin

children > 2 yr), profound lethargy, significant weight loss

= Gl obstruction or disease: bilious vomit, projectile vomit, hematemesis, hematochezia, marked
abdominal distension, peritoneal signs
neurological or systemic disease: bulging fontanelle, headache, positional triggers, altered
consciousness, focal neurological deficits, history or signs of trauma, hypotension disproportionate to
apparent illness, hyponatremia with hyperkalemia
remember that vomiting without diarrhea is not always gastroenteritis (e.g. DKA)

= post-tussive vomiting is also common with coughing fits in children

Physical

+ vital signs to determine clinical status and hydration state (refer to Table 19, P40)
+ abdominal examination for evidence of obstruction or focal tenderness

+ neurologic assessment for signs of increased ICP

Investigations

+ investigations should be guided by presentation (history & physical) and differential diagnoses

+ ifchild appears well and no worrisome features, often investigation is not required

+ ifinvestigations are warranted, the following may be done: CBC, electrolytes, BUN, Cr, amylase, lipase,
glucose, liver enzymes, urinalysis

+ insick child, add: CRP, venous blood gases, C&S (blood, stool), imaging (x-ray, U/S)
