BENIGN VULVAR LESIONS

Non-Neoplastic Disorders of Vulvar Epithelium
+ biopsy is often necessary to make diagnosis and/or rule out malignancy:
1. Lichen sclerosus
— subepithelial fat becomes diminished; labia become thin, atrophic, with membrane-like
epithelium and labial fusion
— pruritus, dyspareunia, burning, bleeding, ulceration, excoriations
— ‘figure of 8’ distribution
— most common in postmenopausal patients but can occur at any age
— patients should be monitored for malignancy, due to increased risk of SCC
— treatment: high-potency topical steroid ointment (clobetasol), likely long-term treatment
necessary
2. Lichen simplex chronicus
— surface of labia majora is thickened and hyperkeratotic, leather-like in appearance
— most common symptoms: pruritus and burning, often at night
— typically occurs in postmenopausal patients
— treatment: medium-potency steroid ointment based on symptom severity + nighttime
antihistamines
3. Lichen planus
— autoimmune disorder where T cells attack basal keratinocytes
— peak incidence at age 30-60 (e)
— 3 variants including erosive, papulosquamous, and hypertrophic
— can extend into vaginal canal and cause loss of structure (desquamative vaginitis) fears on
— can have oral lichen planus in oral cavity es
— treatment: ultrapotent steroid ointment BID until plaques resolve, vaginal suppositories,
or immunosuppressive therapies (e.g. cyclosporine) are all accepted

Tumours
+ papillary hidradenoma, nevus, fibroma, hemangioma

MALIGNANT VULVAR LESIONS

Epidemiology
+ 5% of genital tract malignancies
+ 90% SCC; remainder melanomas, basal cell carcinoma, Paget's disease, Bartholin’s gland carcinoma
= Type I disease: HPV-related (50-70%), usual or classical vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia VIN
+ more likely in younger patients
+ 90% of VIN contain HPV DNA (usually types 16, 18)
= Typelll disease: not HPV-related, associated with current or previous vulvar dystrophy, differentiated
VIN
* usually postmenopausal patients
+ related to lichen sclerosis

Risk Factors
+ HPVinfection
+ VIN: precancerous change which presents as multicentric white or pigmented plaques on vulva (may only
be visible at colposcopy)
progression to cancer rarely occurs with appropriate management
= treatment: local excision (i.e. superficial vulvectomy + split thickness skin grafting to cover defects if
required) vs. ablative therapy (i.e. laser, cauterization) vs. local immunotherapy (imiquimod)
history of cervical cancer
cigarette smoking
immunodeficiency
vulvar lichen sclerosus
