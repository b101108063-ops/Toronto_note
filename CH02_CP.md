# Clinical Pharmacology

## General Principles

### Drug Nomenclature

- **DIN (Drug Identification Number)**: assigned to each drug approved by Health Canada
- **NDC (National Drug Code)**: assigned by FDA (US), equivalent to DIN in Canada
- **DIN-HM**: identification number for registered homeopathic products in Canada
- **NPN (Natural Product Number)**: refers to natural health products regulated by the Natural and Non-Prescription Health Products Directorate (NNHPD) within Health Canada
- **Chemical name**: describes chemical structure; consistent internationally via IUPAC (e.g., N-(4-hydroxyphenyl)acetamide = acetaminophen)
- **Non-proprietary (generic) name**: approved/official name listed in pharmacopeia; may contain a class-specific ending (e.g., atorvastatin, pravastatin, simvastatin)
- **Proprietary (trade) name**: brand name or registered trademark (e.g., Lipitor)

### Phases of Clinical Drug Testing

- **Pre-clinical**: laboratory studies in cells, tissues, organs, or animals to examine PK and PD properties and potential toxicities
- **Phase I**: first administration to a small number of healthy volunteers; to explore PK and safety
- **Phase II**: first administration to patients; to determine efficacy, safety, dose range, and PK
- **Phase III**: comparative studies (new drug vs. placebo or standard of care) in larger patient groups; double-blinded RCTs to establish safety and efficacy
- **Phase IV**: post-marketing surveillance; to determine effectiveness, monitor long-term drug effects, and detect previously unappreciated ADRs

## Pharmacokinetics (PK)

Pharmacokinetics = "what the body does to the drug" — the processes and rates of drug absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion from the body following drug administration.

### Absorption

**Definition**: movement of the drug from the site of administration into the circulating bloodstream.

#### Mechanisms of Drug Absorption

- **Passive diffusion** across the lipid bilayer of a cell membrane (most common)
- **Active transport**
- **Facilitated diffusion**
- **Pinocytosis/phagocytosis**

#### Factors Affecting the Rate and Extent of Drug Absorption

- **Lipophilicity** (P<sub>o/w</sub>)
- **Local blood flow** at the site of administration (e.g., sublingual vessels facilitate rapid absorption)
- **Total surface area** for absorption (e.g., small intestinal villi have a large surface area and are the primary site of absorption for most orally-administered drugs)
- **Molecular size** (e.g., drugs with large molecular weights >1000 Da are not as easily absorbed by passive diffusion)
- **pH and drug ionization**
  - Drugs are usually weak acids (e.g., ASA) or weak bases (e.g., ketoconazole) and exist in ionized and non-ionized forms
  - Non-ionized (uncharged) forms cross cell membranes more readily by passive diffusion than ionized (charged) forms
  - The ratio of ionized to non-ionized forms is determined by body compartment pH and drug pKa (Henderson-Hasselbalch equation)
- **Drug transporters** (e.g., P-glycoprotein, organic anion transporting polypeptides)

### Bioavailability

**Definition**: proportion of dose that reaches systemic circulation in an unchanged state that is available to access the site of action.

- Lower F usually reflects limited drug absorption or significant presystemic clearance (e.g., first-pass effect, substrate for drug transporters)
- **IV dose has 100% bioavailability (F=1)**

#### First-Pass Effect

**Definition**: metabolism (biotransformation) of the drug prior to reaching systemic circulation, resulting in reduced F.

- Can occur with PO administration: GI tract > portal vein > liver (possible first-pass metabolism) > systemic circulation
- With rectal administration, approximately 50% of drug absorbed goes through the portal system

### Drug Transporters

- Many drug transporters affect uptake or efflux of drugs from cells and organelles, affecting absorption, distribution, and excretion
- **P-glycoprotein (P-gp)** is a transport protein of clinical relevance:
  - Found in small intestinal epithelium, proximal tubule, bile canaliculi, and BBB
  - Acts as a multidrug efflux pump and provides a natural defence mechanism against drugs and xenobiotics
  - Opposes absorption and enhances elimination of many P-gp substrates (e.g., digoxin, etoposide, paclitaxel, tacrolimus, cyclosporine, apixaban)
  - **P-gp inhibitors** (e.g., most macrolide antibiotics): lead to increased serum concentrations of P-gp substrates
  - **P-gp inducers** (e.g., rifampin, St. John's wort): increase efflux activity, leading to decreased serum concentrations of P-gp substrates
  - Some tumours overexpress P-gp, leading to multidrug resistance to chemotherapeutic agents
- Other members of the **ATP Binding Cassette (ABC)** superfamily and **Solute Carrier (SLC)** superfamily also affect drug absorption; SLC members generally function as uptake drug transporters

### Distribution

**Definition**: movement of drugs between different body compartments, including their sites of action.

- Major body fluid compartments: plasma, interstitial fluid, intracellular fluid, and transcellular fluid (e.g., CSF, peritoneal, pleural)
- Tissue compartments include fat, muscle, and brain

#### Factors Affecting the Rate and Extent of Drug Distribution

- Physical and chemical properties of the drug (e.g., P<sub>o/w</sub>, pKa, molecular weight)
- pH of fluid
- Binding to plasma proteins
- Binding within compartments (depots such as heart muscle, bone, fat tissue)
- Regional blood flow
- Drug transporters

#### Plasma Protein Binding

- Some drug molecules bind to plasma proteins in the blood and exist in equilibrium of two forms:
  1. **Bound to plasma protein**: acidic drugs bind to albumin; basic drugs bind to alpha-1-acid glycoprotein
  2. **Free (unbound)**: can leave the circulation to distribute into tissues and exert an effect, subject to metabolism and elimination
- Bound fraction is determined by drug concentration, binding affinity, and plasma protein concentration (number of binding sites)
- Reduced number of binding sites (e.g., hypoalbuminemia) or saturation of binding sites may result in increased concentration of free drug, which is often cleared with no harmful effects, although toxicity is possible

#### Volume of Distribution (Vd)

- Vd = the apparent volume of fluid into which a drug distributes
- **Calculated value**: Vd = amount of drug in body (dose administered) / initial plasma drug concentration
- A theoretical value that does not correspond to an actual physiologic volume; Vd can greatly exceed TBW
- **Total Body Water (TBW)** represents the maximal anatomical fluid volume thought to be accessible to a drug (~40 L for average adult)
- **Small Vd** (e.g., <0.04 L/kg): corresponds to a drug that distributes in plasma and/or binds plasma proteins to a high degree
- **Large Vd**: corresponds to a drug that preferentially distributes into tissues (e.g., fat, muscle); since most of the drug is not located in blood, the measured drug concentration in blood is low; thus Vd "appears" large
- Vd of drugs that are highly bound to plasma proteins can be altered by liver and kidney disease
- Vd changes with age; in the geriatric population, as there is often a reduction in total body water and total muscle mass, but an increase in total body fat, there is an increase in the Vd of hydrophobic drugs

#### Depot

- A body compartment in which drug molecules tend to be stored and released slowly over a long period of time
- **Fat** serves as a depot for very lipid soluble drugs (e.g., diazepam, THC)
- Some oil-based medications are injected IM for slow release (e.g., depot medroxyprogesterone acetate dosed q3mo; depot risperidone dosed q2wk)

#### Barriers (Relative)

- Anatomical structures that limit or prevent diffusion of drug molecules, such as the **placenta** or **BBB** (composed of tight junctions between capillary endothelial cells and astrocytes)
- Physiological barriers such as drug transporters (e.g., P-gp), often serve as a natural defence mechanism against drugs and xenobiotics
- Consider dosing route and/or drug interactions for drug penetration across these barriers
- Barriers are important in determining sites of action and side effect profiles (e.g., risk of CNS depression if drug crosses BBB, risk of harm to a fetus if drug crosses placenta)

### Metabolism (Biotransformation)

**Definition**: chemical transformation of a drug in vivo.

- Sites of biotransformation: liver (main), GI tract, lung, plasma, kidney, and most other tissues
- Results of biotransformation:
  - A **prodrug** (inactive drug) may be activated (e.g., tamoxifen to endoxifen; codeine to morphine)
  - A drug may be changed to another **active metabolite** (e.g., diazepam to oxazepam)
  - A drug may be changed to a **toxic metabolite** (e.g., acetaminophen to NAPQI)
  - A drug may be **inactivated** (e.g., acetaminophen to acetaminophen glucuronide)

#### Drug Metabolizing Pathways

**Phase I Reactions**
- Oxidation, reduction, or hydrolysis reactions that introduce or unmask polar groups on a parent compound to slightly increase water solubility (e.g., hydroxylation, demethylation)
- The change in P<sub>o/w</sub> is typically minimal compared to Phase II; often Phase I places a polar "handle" on a hydrophobic drug to permit conjugation in Phase II
- Often mediated by **cytochrome P450 (CYP)** enzymes found in the endoplasmic reticulum (primarily in hepatocytes)
- Products of the reaction can be excreted or undergo further Phase II reactions

**Phase II Reactions (Conjugation)**
- Conjugation with large endogenous substrates that are often polar (e.g., glucuronic acid, glutathione, sulphate, acetyl groups, methyl groups, amino acids)
- Often substantially increases water solubility and renal elimination
- Can result in biologically active metabolites (e.g., morphine glucuronide)
- Can occur independently of Phase I reactions (e.g., morphine to morphine glucuronides)

#### Factors Affecting Drug Biotransformation

- **Genetic polymorphisms** of metabolizing enzymes:
  - Individual genotypes may alter the rate of drug metabolism (poor, intermediate, extensive, or ultra-rapid metabolizers; extensive is considered "normal")
  - May lead to toxicity (if poor metabolizer) or ineffectiveness (if ultra-rapid metabolizer)
  - Examples: tamoxifen, tramadol, and codeine are prodrugs activated by CYP2D6; warfarin is metabolized by CYP2C9 (loss-of-function alleles lead to higher drug concentrations)
- **Enzyme inhibition** from other chemical exposures including drugs and foods:
  - CYP inhibition leads to increased concentration and bioavailability of the substrate drug (e.g., erythromycin CYP3A4 inhibitor can predispose to simvastatin toxicity)
  - **Grapefruit juice** is a potent inhibitor of intestinal CYP3A4, resulting in markedly reduced first-pass metabolism (e.g., saquinavir AUC increased 3-fold, simvastatin AUC increased 17-fold)
- **Enzyme induction** may be due to the same or other medications:
  - Certain medications enhance gene transcription to increase metabolizing enzyme activity
  - A drug may induce its own metabolism (e.g., carbamazepine) or that of other drugs (e.g., phenobarbital can induce OCP metabolism)
- **Liver dysfunction** (e.g., hepatitis, alcoholic liver disease, biliary cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma) may decrease drug metabolism
- **Renal disease** often results in decreased drug metabolism
- **Extremes of age** (neonates or elderly) have reduced biotransformation capacity; toddlers often have increased capacity
- **Nutrition**: insufficient protein and fatty acid intake decreases biotransformation; vitamin/mineral deficiencies may also impact metabolizing enzymes
- **Alcohol**: acute ingestion inhibits CYP2E1; chronic consumption can induce CYP2E1, increasing risk of hepatocellular damage from acetaminophen by increasing NAPQI production
- **Smoking** can induce CYP1A2, thus increasing metabolism of some drugs (e.g., theophylline, clozapine)

### Excretion

**Definition**: removal of a drug from the body.

#### Routes of Drug Excretion

**Kidney** (main organ of excretion)
- Renal drug clearance = (glomerular filtration + tubular secretion) - (tubular reabsorption)
- Renal function (assessed using serum creatinine) decreases with age (7.5 mL/min per decade) and is affected by many disease states
- Processes affecting renal excretion:
  1. **Glomerular filtration**: passive process; only the free drug fraction can be eliminated; filtration rate depends on GFR, degree of protein binding, and size of drug
  2. **Tubular secretion**: saturable transport process allowing both protein-bound and free drug fractions to be excreted; distinct transport mechanisms for weak acids (e.g., penicillin, salicylic acid, probenecid, chlorothiazide) and weak bases (e.g., quinine, metformin); drugs may block the secretion of other drugs if they share or inhibit the same drug transporter (e.g., probenecid inhibits penicillin excretion via organic anion transporters)
  3. **Tubular reabsorption**: some drugs can be actively or passively reabsorbed back into the systemic circulation, reducing excretion

**Stool**
- Some drugs and metabolites are actively secreted into bile or directly into the GI tract
- **Enterohepatic recirculation**: drugs are reabsorbed from the intestine and returned to the liver, cycling between intestine and liver, prolonging drug duration (e.g., some glucuronic acid conjugates excreted in bile may be hydrolyzed in intestines to their original form and reabsorbed)

**Lungs**
- Elimination of anesthetic gases and vapours by exhalation

**Other routes**
- Sweat, saliva, and breast milk are generally less significant routes
- Saliva concentrations of some drugs parallel their plasma concentrations (e.g., rifampin)
- Drug excretion into breast milk is a concern for some drugs

## Pharmacokinetic Considerations

### Time Course of Drug Action

- Absorption, distribution, and elimination can be graphically represented (e.g., concentration vs. time graph)
- Many kinetic parameters are measured after IV dosing to accurately account for absorption and distribution
- Drugs such as warfarin can exhibit **hysteresis** (for a single drug concentration, there may be two different response levels)

### Half-Life

**Definition**: time taken for the serum drug concentration to decrease by 50%; usually referred to as the elimination half-life.

- Applies only to drugs with first-order kinetics (i.e., most drugs)
- Approximately four to five half-lives are required to reach steady-state with repeated dosing, or for complete drug elimination once dosing is stopped

### Steady-State Drug Concentration

- Drug concentration remains constant when the amount of drug entering the system is equivalent to the amount eliminated
- Determination of drug concentrations in TDM is of greatest utility when steady-state has been reached
- **Special dosing situations**:
  - Use a **loading dose** for drugs with a long half-life and/or when there is a clinical need to rapidly achieve therapeutic levels (e.g., amiodarone, digoxin, phenytoin, some antibiotic settings)
  - Use **continuous infusion** for drugs with a very short half-life and when there is a need for a long-term effect (e.g., nitroprusside, unfractionated heparin, naloxone)

### Clearance (Cl)

- Quantitative measurement of the volume of body fluid from which a substance is removed per unit time
- **Cl = rate of elimination of drug / plasma drug concentration**
- Represents total body clearance rate, unless specified for a particular organ (e.g., liver or kidney)
- Total body clearance is determined from the sum of individual clearance rates or by determining k<sub>e</sub> x Vd, where k<sub>e</sub> is the elimination rate constant equal to In 2/half-life

### Elimination Kinetics

**First-Order Kinetics** (most common)
- Constant proportion of drug eliminated per unit time
- Some drugs can follow first-order kinetics until elimination is saturated (usually at large doses), at which point Cl is less than predicted for a given concentration
- Shows linear relationship when plotted on a graph of concentration (log) vs. time (linear)

**Non-Linear or Zero-Order Kinetics** (less common)
- Applies to a few drugs in the therapeutic range (e.g., alcohol, phenytoin, Aspirin) and is more commonly associated with overdose
- Constant amount of drug eliminated per unit time, regardless of concentration; the concept of half-life does not apply
- Saturation of various ADME processes creates non-linear kinetics; first-order exhibited at lower concentrations and zero-order at higher concentrations after saturation
- Drug-specific nomograms aid clinicians in dosing; these drugs are often targets of TDM (e.g., phenytoin, theophylline)

### Loading and Maintenance Doses

- **Loading doses** are used when an immediate effect is needed; parenteral administration is the most common way to "fill up" the volume of distribution for drugs that distribute extensively outside the vascular compartment
- Steady-state levels are achieved after approximately four to five half-lives
- **Maintenance doses** can be given as intermittent oral doses (common) or continuous infusion (rare)

## Pharmacodynamics (PD)

**Definition**: study of the effects of the drug on the body.

### Dose-Response Relationship

**Graded dose-response relationship**: relates dose to intensity of effect.

#### Efficacy

- The relative ability of a drug to produce a maximal (biological/biochemical) response
- Measured by **E<sub>max</sub>** (the maximal response that a drug can elicit)

#### Potency

- Measured by **EC<sub>50</sub>** (the concentration of a drug needed to produce 50% of E<sub>max</sub>); **ED<sub>50</sub>** if dose is used
- A drug that reaches its EC<sub>50</sub> at a lower concentration is more potent

### Effects of Drugs on Receptors

#### Agonists

- Drugs that mimic the effects of the endogenous ligand and evoke a response when bound to the receptor
- **Affinity**: the ability and strength of the agonist to bind to the receptor
- **Efficacy**: the ability to elicit a response via the receptor interaction
- Drug efficacy is often determined under ideal conditions; drug effectiveness is a better measure of how the drug works in real-world situations
- **Full agonists**: can elicit a maximal effect at a receptor (e.g., methadone or morphine on the mu opioid receptor system)
- **Partial agonists**: can only elicit a partial effect, irrespective of concentration; also known as a ceiling effect (e.g., buprenorphine on the mu opioid receptor system)

#### Antagonists

- Drugs that reduce the action of an agonist drug or of an endogenous ligand
- **Chemical antagonism**: direct chemical interaction between agonist and antagonist prevents agonist-receptor binding (e.g., chelating agents for heavy metals)
- **Physiological/functional antagonism**: drugs that produce opposite physiological effects (e.g., insulin decreases blood glucose vs. glucagon raises blood glucose)
- **Pharmacological antagonism**: antagonist binds to the same site as the agonist or an alternative effector site and reduces the ability of the agonist to bind
  - **Competitive antagonism**: antagonist binds directly to the active site on a given receptor, without activating it (zero efficacy), and prevents the agonist from binding
    - **Reversible competitive antagonists**: bind non-covalently; increasing concentrations of agonist may overcome the antagonist (e.g., naloxone is a competitive antagonist to morphine or heroin)
    - **Irreversible competitive antagonists**: form a covalent bond with the receptor and cannot be displaced, irreversibly blocking other substrates from binding
  - **Non-competitive antagonism**: antagonist (negative allosteric modulator) binds to an alternate site distinct from the active site, producing allosteric effects that reduce the ability of the agonist to bind (e.g., phenoxybenzamine forms a covalent bond with adrenergic receptors preventing adrenaline and NE from binding)

### Effectiveness and Safety

**ED<sub>50</sub> (Effective Dose)**: the dose of a drug needed to elicit a therapeutic effect in 50% of a test population (derived from quantal dose-response curves).

**LD<sub>50</sub> (Lethal Dose)**: the dose of a drug needed to cause death in 50% of a test population.

**TD<sub>50</sub> (Toxic Dose)**: the dose of a drug that causes a harmful effect in 50% of a test population.

#### Therapeutic Index

**Therapeutic Index**: TD<sub>50</sub>/ED<sub>50</sub> (LD<sub>50</sub>/ED<sub>50</sub>, commonly derived from non-human studies).

- A measure of relative drug safety; the larger the TI, the safer the drug
- Common drugs with a narrow therapeutic window or low TI that sometimes require TDM: digoxin, theophylline, warfarin, lithium, cyclosporine
- Factors that can change the TI: interacting drugs, changes in drug ADME, patient characteristics (e.g., age, pregnancy, organ impairment)

#### Certain Safety Factor

**Certain Safety Factor**: TD<sub>1</sub>/ED<sub>99</sub>
- Comparison of the dose that is effective in at least 99% of the population and toxic in less than 1% of the population
- Regulatory agencies often require a certain safety factor or margin of safety above 100

## Variability in Drug Response

- Recommended patient dosing is based on clinical research representing mean values for a select population, but each person may be unique in their dosing requirements due to age, genetics, disease states, drug interactions, diet, environmental factors, etc.

### Possible Causes of Individual Variability in Drug Response

- **Intake**: medication adherence
- **Absorption**: vomiting, diarrhea, or steatorrhea; first-pass effect increased due to enzyme induction or decreased due to enzyme inhibition or liver disease; pharmacokinetic or pharmacodynamic drug interactions (e.g., calcium carbonate complexes with iron, thyroxine, and fluoroquinolones in the GI lumen)
- **Distribution**: very high or low percentage body fat; intact or disrupted BBB; elderly or neonate; liver dysfunction
- **Biotransformation and elimination**: genetic polymorphisms or enzyme deficiencies related to drug metabolism (e.g., CYP polymorphism); kidney or liver dysfunction
- **PD**: genetic variability in drug response (e.g., immune-mediated reactions); diseases that affect drug PD; drug tolerance or cross-tolerance

## Therapeutic Drug Monitoring (TDM)

**Definition**: using serum drug concentration data to optimize drug therapy (e.g., dose adjustment, monitor compliance). Serum drug samples are usually taken when steady-state has been reached (~4-5 half-lives).

- TDM is often used for drugs that have low TIs, unpredictable dose-response relationships, significant consequences associated with therapeutic failure or toxicity, and/or wide inter-patient PK variability. Most drugs do not require TDM.
- Nomograms are often used for low TI drugs, particularly in patients with renal insufficiency, hepatic failure, dialysis, and hypoalbuminemia

**Examples of drugs that sometimes require TDM**:
- Vancomycin
- Aminoglycosides (gentamicin, tobramycin)
- Digoxin
- Phenytoin and other anticonvulsants
- Warfarin
- Lithium

## Adverse Drug Reactions

**ADE (Adverse Drug Event)**: events that occur while a patient is on a drug at either appropriate or inappropriate dosage. A causal relationship is not required.

**ADR (Adverse Drug Reaction)**: reactions to drugs that occur when a drug is used for the appropriate indication at normal therapeutic doses.

### Approach to Suspected Adverse Drug Reactions

- **History and physical exam**: signs and symptoms of reaction (e.g., rash, fever, hepatitis, anaphylaxis), timing, risk factors, detailed medication history including all drugs and timing, de-challenge (response when drug is removed), and re-challenge (response when drug is given again, if applicable)
- **Medication history** should include prescription, non-prescription and OTC, natural health products/samples, supplements, creams, ear/eye drops, inhalers, and nasal sprays
- Dosage, frequency, route of administration, and duration of use should be recorded for each
- Differentiate between drug therapy vs. disease pathophysiology
- **Treatment of serious ADRs**: stop the drug, supportive care, and symptomatic relief; specific interventions (e.g., steroids, immunosuppressants) used for some ADRs
- **Resources**: check recent literature, Health Canada, and FDA; contact the pharmaceutical company; call Poison Control (Canada: 1-844-POISON-X; regional: 1-800-268-9017); check MotherToBaby for pregnant/breastfeeding women
- **Report all suspected ADRs** that are: 1) unexpected, 2) serious, or 3) reactions to recently marketed drugs (<5 yr on market) regardless of nature or severity

## Drug Interactions

- **Definition**: concomitant medications (including natural health products, e.g., St. John's wort) or foods (e.g., grapefruit juice) alter the effect of one or more other drugs by changing PK and/or PD

### Pharmacokinetic (PK) Interactions

Involve changes in drug concentration when a new drug is added:
- **Absorption**: alterations in GI pH, gastric mucosa and/or emptying, intestinal motility, and/or transporter function
- **Distribution**: alterations in blood flow, plasma protein binding, anatomical and/or functional barriers (e.g., drug transporters)
- **Metabolism**: alterations in drug metabolism
- **Excretion**: alterations in renal or hepatic excretion

### Pharmacodynamic (PD) Interactions

- Due to two or more drugs that exert similar effects (additive or synergistic) or opposing effects (antagonistic)

## Autonomic Nervous System (ANS)

- Most organs are innervated by both sympathetic and parasympathetic nerves, which have opposing effects
- **ACh** and **NE** are the main neurotransmitters of the autonomic NS
- ACh binds to many cholinergic receptor subtypes: nicotinic and muscarinic receptors
- NE binds to adrenergic receptors: principally **alpha1**, **alpha2**, **beta1**, **beta2**
- ACh action is terminated by metabolism in the synaptic cleft by **acetylcholinesterase** and in plasma by **pseudocholinesterase**
- **Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors** (pyridostigmine, donepezil, galantamine, rivastigmine) can increase ACh levels in conditions such as myasthenia gravis or Alzheimer disease
- NE action is terminated by reuptake at the presynaptic membrane, diffusion from the synaptic cleft, and degradation by **MAO** and **COMT**

### Parasympathetic Nervous System (PNS) — "Rest and Digest"

- Blood vessels, sweat glands, spleen capsule, and adrenal medulla do NOT have parasympathetic innervation
- Parasympathetic pre-ganglionic fibres originate in the lower brainstem from cranial nerves III, VII, IX, and X, and in the sacral spinal cord at levels S2-S4 ("craniosacral" nervous system)
- They connect with post-ganglionic fibres via **nicotinic receptors** in ganglionic cells located near or within the target organ
- Post-ganglionic fibres connect with effector tissues via:
  - **M1 muscarinic receptors**: located in the CNS
  - **M2 muscarinic receptors**: located in smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, and glandular epithelium

### Sympathetic Nervous System (SNS) — "Fight or Flight"

- Sympathetic pre-ganglionic fibres originate in the spinal cord at spinal levels T1-L2/L3 ("thoracolumbar" nervous system)
- Pre-ganglionic fibres connect with postganglionic fibres via **nicotinic receptors** located in one of two groups of ganglia:
  1. Paravertebral ganglia (sympathetic trunk) that lie in a chain close to the vertebral column
  2. Prevertebral ganglia (celiac and mesenteric ganglia) that lie within the abdomen
- Post-ganglionic fibres connect with effector tissues via:
  - **Beta1 receptors**: in cardiac tissue
  - **Beta2 receptors**: in smooth muscle of bronchi and GI tract
  - **Alpha1 receptors**: in vascular smooth muscle
  - **Alpha2 receptors**: in vascular smooth muscle
  - **M3 muscarinic receptors**: located in sweat glands

## Opioid Therapy and Chronic Non-Cancer Pain

### General Management Principles

- When first considering therapy for patients with chronic non-cancer pain, optimize non-opioid pharmacotherapy and non-pharmacologic therapy rather than starting a trial of opioids
- General approaches to opioid use include avoiding high doses, and when possible, a slow, collaborative approach when tapering
- For patients beginning opioid therapy, restrict prescribed dose to **<90 MME/day**, and ideally **<50 MME/day**, especially at starting dose
- For patients currently using >=90 MME/day, encourage a slow, collaborative taper to the lowest effective dose
- For patients experiencing serious challenges in tapering, a formal multidisciplinary program is suggested
- Refer to national opioid guidelines (https://www.cmaj.ca/content/189/18/E659)
