# 1. Healthcare System and Law

## 1.1 Division of Government Responsibilities

The Canadian healthcare system involves shared federal and provincial responsibilities.

**Canada Health Act - Five Criteria:**

- **Comprehensiveness**: provincial plans must cover all medically necessary services
- **Universality**: all eligible residents must be entitled to healthcare services on uniform terms
- **Portability**: emergency health services must be available to Canadians outside their home province
- **Accessibility**: plans must ensure reasonable access to healthcare without financial or other barriers
- **Public Administration**: insurance plans must be administered on a non-profit basis by a public authority

## 1.2 Legal Foundation

**Five Constitutional Documents:**

1. **Royal Proclamation (1763)**: Foundation for Indigenous rights in Canada; sets out sovereignty of Indigenous peoples
2. **Constitution Act (1867)**: Deals primarily with jurisdictional power between federal and provincial governments
3. **Treaty 6 (1876)**: Included Medicine Chest Clause addressing Indigenous sovereignty in healthcare delivery
4. **Dreaver vs. King (1935)**: Legal precedence for Non-Insured Health Benefits
5. **Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms (1982)**: If government finances healthcare, must do so consistent with Charter rights

**Two Key Statutes:**

- **Canada Health Act (1984)**: Outlines national terms and conditions provincial health systems must meet for federal transfer payments
- **Canada Health and Social Transfer Act (1996)**: Federal grant for healthcare, social programs, and post-secondary education

## 1.4 Healthcare Expenditure and Delivery

- Total healthcare expenditure in 2023: $344 billion ($8,740 per person)
- Health spending: 12.1% of Canada's GDP

**Sources of Healthcare Funding:**
- Public sector covers fee-for-service, capitation, or Alternate Payment Plans (APP) in hospitals
- Public sector does NOT cover: dentists, chiropractors, optometrists, massage therapists, physiotherapists, podiatrists, psychologists, prescription drugs, OTC drugs, personal health supplies, residential care facilities

**Healthcare Delivery:**
- Hospital services publicly funded but delivered through private, not-for-profit institutions
- Owned and operated by communities, religious organizations, and regional health authorities

# 2. Ethics Principles

## 2.1 Introduction to Ethics

**Ethics involves:**
1. Principles and values to consider what is morally permissible or impermissible
2. Rights, duties, and obligations of individuals and groups
3. Trust in the physician-patient relationship (fiduciary duty)

**Fiduciary Duty:**
- Legal duty to act in patient's interest
- Profit must be strictly accounted for
- Improper profit results in sanctions and potential compensation

## 2.2 Four Principles Approach (Principlism)

<table>
<tr><th>Principle</th><th>Definition</th></tr>
<tr><td>**Autonomy</td><td>Individual's right to make decisions according to their values, beliefs, and preferences. Not synonymous with capacity.</td></tr>
<tr><td>**Beneficence</td><td>Obligation to provide benefit based on patient's best interests, considering their values and preferences.</td></tr>
<tr><td>**Non-Maleficence</td><td>Obligation to avoid causing harm ("primum non nocere" - first, do no harm).</td></tr>
<tr><td>**Justice</td><td>Fair distribution of benefits and harms regardless of geography or income. Includes equity, equality, and human rights.</td></tr>
</table>

**Key Distinctions:**
- **Autonomy vs. Capacity**: Autonomy is a right; capacity is an ability
- **Equality vs. Equity**: Equality distributes resources equally; equity considers differing needs

## 2.3 CMA Code of Ethics and Professionalism

**Virtues of the Ethical Physician:**
- Compassion, Integrity, Honesty, Prudence, Humility

**Fundamental Commitments:**
- Commitment to the well-being of the patient
- Commitment to professional excellence
- Commitment to respect for persons
- Commitment to self-care and peer support
- Commitment to justice
- Commitment to inquiry and reflection
- Commitment to professional integrity and competence

**Professional Responsibilities:**
- Patient-physician relationship
- Decision-making
- Managing conflicts of interest
- Responsibilities to self, colleagues, and society

# 3. Consent and Capacity

## 3.1 Overview of Consent

**Key Principles:**
- Consent must be informed, given voluntarily, and obtained by a capable person
- For minors: consent by parent/guardian or immature minor (if capable)
- For adults lacking capacity: substitute decision-maker (SDM) provides consent

## 3.2 Elements of Informed Consent

1. **Disclosure**: Information about diagnosis, nature of treatment, risks and benefits, alternatives
2. **Understanding**: Patient must comprehend the information
3. **Voluntary**: No coercion or undue influence
4. **Capacity**: Patient must be legally capable of making the decision

## 3.3 Capacity Assessment

**Criteria for Capacity:**
- Patient must understand the information provided
- Patient must appreciate how the information applies to their situation
- Patient must reason about the options
- Patient must express a choice

**Presumption:**
- All adults are presumed capable unless proven otherwise
- Burden of proof lies with the healthcare provider to demonstrate incapacity

# 4. Confidentiality and Privacy

## 4.1 Overview of Confidentiality

**Foundational Assumption:**
- All information given by the patient is confidential (cannot be disclosed)
- Information is privileged (cannot be used in court)

**Key Points:**
- Privacy is a right of patients (may be waived)
- Confidentiality is a duty of physicians (must be respected)
- Physicians should not promise absolute confidentiality as it cannot be guaranteed by law

## 4.2 Legal Basis for Confidentiality

**Governed by:**
- Provincial health information legislation
- Common law court decisions
- CMA Code of Ethics and Professionalism

**Consequences of Breach:**
- Sanctions by hospital, court, or regulatory authority
- May result in compensation to patient

## 4.3 Statutory Reporting Obligations

Physicians must report in specific circumstances where public interest overrides confidentiality:

<table>
<tr><th>Situation</th><th>Report To</th></tr>
<tr><td>Suspected child abuse or neglect</td><td>Children's Aid Society</td></tr>

<tr><td>Communicable diseases</td><td>Local public health authority</td></tr>
<tr><td>Improper conduct of other physicians</td><td>College or regulatory body</td></tr>
<tr><td>Vital statistics (births, deaths)</td><td>Office of Registrar General or municipal authorities</td></tr>
<tr><td>Reportable deaths (violent, suspicious, MAID)</td><td>Coroner</td></tr>

# 5. End-of-Life Care

## 5.1 Palliative Care

**Definition:**
- Approach improving quality of life for patients with life-threatening illness
- Prevents and relieves suffering through pain management, physical, psychosocial, and spiritual care

**Key Points:**
- Early palliative care improves quality of life, reduces aggressive treatments, and may extend survival
- Appropriate for any patient at any stage of serious illness
- May occur in hospital, hospice, community, or home
- Often involves interdisciplinary team
- **Palliative sedation**: Use of sedatives for terminally ill to relieve suffering

**Withdrawing or Withholding Life-Sustaining Interventions:**
- Distinct from euthanasia/MAID
- Considered allowing natural death from underlying condition
- Consent required from capable patient or SDM

## 5.2 Medical Assistance in Dying (MAID)

**Definition:**
- Medical practitioner or nurse practitioner administers or prescribes a substance causing death at patient's request

**Legal Framework:**

- **Carter v. Canada (2015)**: Supreme Court ruled criminal prohibition on physician-assisted death unconstitutional
- **Bill C-14 (2016)**: Legalized MAID with eligibility criteria and safeguards
- **Bill C-39 (2023)**: Temporarily postponed MAID for mental illness only
- **Bill C-62 (2024)**: Extended postponement of MAID for mental illness to March 2027

**Bill C-14 Eligibility Criteria:**
- Eligible for publicly-funded health services in Canada
- At least 18 years old with capacity for clear, freely given consent
- Grievous and irremediable medical condition with advanced state of irreversible decline
- Suffering intolerable to patient not relieved under acceptable conditions

**MAID Process:**
1. Eligibility criteria satisfied
2. Patient signs written request
3. Two independent witnesses sign request (18+ years, not benefiting from death)
4. Healthcare provider informs patient of right to withdraw consent
5. Two independent assessors confirm eligibility
6. 10 clear days must elapse (unless imminent death or capacity loss)
7. For non-reasonable foreseeable death: minimum 90 days
8. Opportunity to withdraw request throughout process
9. Final consent required just before MAID (unless waived under specific conditions)

**Ethical Considerations:**
- Belief that prohibiting MAID may cause patients to end lives or endure intolerable suffering
- Patient autonomy in decisions about time of death
- No ethical difference between euthanasia/assisted suicide and forgoing life-sustaining treatments
- Death should only occur from withdrawal of life-sustaining treatments in cases of fatal underlying condition

**Key Distinctions:**
- Palliative sedation with opioids (double effect): death may occur as unintended consequence
- Withdrawal of life-support: allowing natural course of underlying disease
- MAID: death is the primary intent

## 5.3 Physician Responsibilities Regarding Death

**Mandatory Reporting to Coroner:**
- Death due to violence, negligence, misconduct, misadventure, or malpractice
- Death during pregnancy or attributable to pregnancy
- Sudden and unexpected death
- Death from untreated disease
- Death from causes other than disease
- Suspicious circumstances
- Death from MAID

**Coroner's Role:**
- Establish identity of deceased, location, time of death
- Determine medical cause and means of death (natural, accidental, suicide, homicide, undetermined)
- Does NOT make decisions regarding criminality or legal responsibility

**Mandatory Referral:**
- CPSO policy (upheld by courts) requires effective referral if physician conscientiously objects to MAID

# 6. Reproductive Ethics

## 6.1 Assisted Human Reproduction

**Assisted Human Reproduction Act (2004) Principles:**
- Health and well-being of children born through ART must be prioritized
- Benefits must secure human health, safety, dignity, and rights
- Women's health and well-being must be protected
- Free and informed consent must be promoted
- No discrimination against persons seeking assisted reproduction
- Trade in reproductive capabilities raises ethical concerns justifying prohibition
- Human individuality, diversity, and genome integrity must be preserved

**Assisted Reproductive Therapies (ART):**
- Non-coital insemination, hormonal ovarian stimulation, IVF
- Ethical considerations include:
  - Donor anonymity vs. child-centered reproduction
  - Preimplantation genetic testing
  - Experimental nature of new techniques
  - Moral status of embryo
  - Access and funding issues
  - Social justice factors (2SLGBTQQIA+ access)

## 6.2 Fetal Tissue and Stem Cells

**Uses of Stem Cells:**
- Studying human development and cell specialization
- Evaluating drug efficacy and safety
- Cell therapy for repair or replacement of tissues
- Genetic treatment of somatic cells

**Oversight:**
- Stem Cell Oversight Committee (part of CIHR) reviews stem cell research

## 6.3 Induced Abortion

**CMA Definition:**
- Active termination of pregnancy before fetal viability (fetus >500g or >20 weeks gestational age)

**Legal Status in Canada:**
- No criminal prohibition regarding abortion
- Decision made between patient and healthcare provider
- 2nd and 3rd trimester abortions legal but usually pursued for health risks or fetal conditions

**Ethical Considerations:**
- Termination is a value-laden topic
- Providers must treat patients requesting termination with dignity and respect
- Conscientious objectors must provide effective referral
- Trust in providers can influence health outcomes

## 6.4 Prenatal Genetic Testing

**Uses:**
1. Confirm clinical diagnosis
2. Detect genetic predisposition to disease
3. Allow preventative steps and preparation
4. Option to terminate pregnancy or begin early treatment

**Ethical Dilemmas:**
- Individual and familial implications
- Future disease identification
- Disorders with no effective treatment
- Fetal sex identification
- Difficulty obtaining truly informed consent due to complexity
- Consent for testing may differ from consent for disclosure
- Duty to maintain confidentiality vs. duty to warn family members
- Risk of psychological harm and social discrimination

**Legal Aspects:**
- Genetic Non-Discrimination Act (2017)
- Requires informed consent
- Physician obligation to inform patients that prenatal testing exists
- Possible breach of confidentiality to warn family members if imminent risk of serious harm

# 7. Medical Errors and Truth-Telling

## 7.1 Disclosure of Medical Errors

**Key Principles:**
- Honesty and transparency with patients
- Acknowledge errors promptly and fully
- Apologize appropriately
- Provide information about what happened and next steps
- Prevent future errors through system improvements

## 7.2 Truth-Telling in Healthcare

**Breaking Bad News:**
- Use a structured approach (e.g., SPIKES protocol)
- Ensure privacy and adequate time
- Assess patient's understanding and desire for information
- Use clear, non-euphemistic language
- Allow time for emotional response
- Provide follow-up and support

**Key Ethical Obligations:**
- Respect patient autonomy
- Truth-telling builds trust
- Patients have right to know about their health
- Consider cultural sensitivities while maintaining honesty

# 8. Professional Regulation

## 8.1 CanMEDS Competencies

**Framework:**
- Professional competencies established by Medical Council of Canada (MCC)
- Objectives for MCC Qualifying Exam

**Key Roles:**
- Medical Expert
- Communicator
- Collaborator
- Health Advocate
- Scholar
- Professional

## 8.2 Physician Conduct

**CPSO Policy on Treating Self and Family:**
- Generally inappropriate to treat self or family for minor conditions
- May treat in emergencies or if no other physician readily available
- Discontinuing needed services without arranging alternatives is professional misconduct

**Sexual Conduct with Patients:**
- Even consensual sexual conduct can lead to battery accusations and professional misconduct
- Includes: intercourse, undue touching, inappropriate sexual references, sexual jokes
- Inappropriate with patients for whom psychotherapy was provided
- Must report colleagues engaging in sexual impropriety

**Record Keeping:**
- Must maintain adequate records demonstrating continuous, comprehensive care
- Standards include: readability, diagnosis, differential diagnosis, appropriate tests and referrals
- Records stored for 10 years in most jurisdictions
- Patient has right to access their medical record

## 8.3 Research Ethics

**Guiding Principles:**
- Respect for persons: informed consent
- Concern for welfare: protecting participants
- Justice: avoiding exploitation and unjustified exclusion

**Requirements:**
- Research Ethics Board (REB) approval required before commencing human research
- Protection of participants is paramount
- Physician researchers must not be primary point of contact for recruiting their own patients
- Tri-Council Policy Statement (TCPS2) provides national standards

**Research with Indigenous People:**
- OCAP principles: Ownership, Control, Access, Possession
- First Nations, Inuit, and Métis (FNIM) communities are self-determining
- Chapter 9 of TCPS2 provides considerations for FNIM research

## 8.4 Physician-Industry Relations

**Key Concerns:**
- Pharmaceutical and healthcare industries are profit-driven (unlike physicians with fiduciary duty)
- Decision-making is influenced by gifts (pens, meals, learn sessions)
- Free product samples associated with increased preference for expensive drugs
- New drugs may not be more effective than standard of care

**Guidelines:**
- Primary goal: advancement of health of Canadians
- Relationships guided by CMA Code of Ethics
- Avoid financial interest in industry prescribing/referral practices
- Maintain professional autonomy and independence
- Commitment to scientific method
- Sponsorship for travel as presenter may be accepted (not just attendance)
- Must disclose conflicts at presentations

# 9. Resource Allocation

## 9.1 Definition and Principles

**Definition:**
- Distribution of resources to programs and people (goods and services)

**Ethical Allocation:**
- If clinically indicated, resources must be made available considering equity and possible discriminatory motivation
- Morally relevant criteria: person's and society's need for and benefit from resources
- Morally irrelevant criteria alone: gender identity, sex, sexual orientation, religion, education, socioeconomic status, age

## 9.2 Ethical Dilemmas in Resource Allocation

- Best outcome vs. fair access for all (e.g., transplant list)
- Aggregation problem: modest benefits to many vs. significant benefits to few
- Fair democratic process for decision-making

## 9.3 Guidelines for Appropriately Allocating Resources

- Protect and promote welfare and best interests of patients
- Consider Indigenous health disparities
- Address negative attitudes based on implicit biases

## 9.4 Indigenous Health and Systemic Barriers

**Over-Representation of Vulnerable Populations:**
- Indigenous peoples face higher rates of child apprehension (52.2% of children in foster care)
- Indigenous women and girls significantly more likely to be murdered or missing
- Indigenous peoples over-represented in incarcerated and homeless populations

**Key Historical Events:**

| Year | Event |
|------|-------|
| 1763 | Royal Proclamation recognizes Indigenous rights and title |
| 1876 | Indian Act gives federal government control over First Nations |
| 1884-1996 | Residential Schools system |
| 1951 | Amendments made to Indian Act |
| 1970s-80s | Indigenous women lobby for equal rights; Bill C-31 passed |
| 1991 | Royal Commission on Aboriginal Peoples (RCAP) |
| 2008 | Prime Minister apologizes for residential schools |
| 2015 | Truth and Reconciliation Commission 94 Calls to Action |
| 2019 | Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women and Girls Final Report |

**Impact of Colonialism:**
- Deliberate cultural erasure through residential schools, Sixties Scoop, Indian hospitals
- Indian Act precluded equal political and economic participation
- Section 6 Indian Status creates two categories of First Nations status
- Non-status peoples lose government support, treaty, and health benefits

## 10.2 Indigenous Health Coverage and Jurisdictions

**Federal Responsibility:**
- Non-Insured Health Benefits (NIHB) program
- Managed by First Nations and Inuit Health Branch of Indigenous Services Canada

**NIHB Coverage:**
- Eye and dental care, pharmacare, transport to medical appointments, mental health counselling
- Strict criteria for coverage
- Only registered First Nations and recognized Inuit eligible

**Barriers to Care:**
- Jurisdictional debates between authorities
- State-imposed definitions of Indigenous identity
- Bureaucratic delays
- Non-status First Nations and Métis denied NIHB

# 11. Clinical Informatics and Ethics

## 11.1 Key Terms

**Health Informatics:**
- Study of information design and use in healthcare

**Clinical Informatics:**
- Application of health informatics in clinical setting to promote quality care
- Three domains: collection of longitudinal health information, exchange of health information, aggregation for analysis

**Digital Health:**
- Use of information technology and electronic communication tools to deliver healthcare
