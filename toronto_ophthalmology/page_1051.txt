Dacryocyst @
Etiology

+ acute or chronic infection of the lacrimal sac

+ most commonly due to obstruction of the nasolacrimal duct

* gram-positive bacteria (e.g. S. aureus, S. pneumoniae): most common cause of acute dacryocystitis in adults

+ gram-negative bacteria (e.g. Pseudomonas species) in immunocompromised patients

‘al Features

pain, swelling, and redness over lacrimal sac below the medial canthus

epiphora, crusting, + fever

digital pressure on the lacrimal sac may express pus through the punctum

in the chronic form, epiphora may be the only symptom of NLD obstruction or dacryocystitis, and the
dacryocystitis may be a clue to an underlying systemic disease

Treatment

+ warm compresses, nasal decongestants, systemic antibiotics (cephalexin if afebrile; cefazolin if febrile)
+ topical antibiotics have limited value as they do not penetrate the site of infection sufficiently

+ I&D; if chronic, obtain cultures by aspiration

+ once infection resolves, consider dacryocystorhinostomy

Dacryoadenitis (a)

Etiology

+ most commonly seen in children and young adults

+ inflammation of the lacrimal gland (outer third of upper eyelid)

* acute causes: S. aureus, mumps, EBV, herpes zoster, N. gonorrhoeae

+ chronic causes (often bilateral): lymphoma, leukemia, sarcoidosis, tuberculosis, TED

ical Features
+ pain, swelling, tearing, discharge, and redness of the outer region of the upper eyelid
+ chronic form is more common and may present as painless enlargement of the lacrimal gland

Treatment

+ supportive: warm compresses, oral NSAIDs
«systemic antibiotics if bacterial cause
